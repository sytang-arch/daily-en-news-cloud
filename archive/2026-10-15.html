5. Webb Telescope Spots Possible Early-Universe Supernova Just Days After Explosion

The James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) has detected a possible supernova in the early universe, just days after the star exploded. Named AT 2026abck, the event was found in a star-forming galaxy at a redshift of 5.017, which means it occurred when the universe was only about 1.2 billion years old. This is one of the youngest supernovas ever observed.

The discovery was made without the help of gravitational lensing, a technique that uses massive objects to magnify distant light. Astronomers say this finding could help them understand how stars lived and died in the early universe. Further observations are planned to confirm the supernova's nature and to study its host galaxy in more detail.

6. Vera Rubin Observatory Spots Largest Ultra-Fast Spinning Asteroid Yet

The Vera Rubin Observatory has discovered the largest asteroid ever seen spinning at an extremely fast rate. The asteroid, which is about 1 kilometer wide, completes a full rotation in just over two hours. This speed is close to the point where the asteroid would break apart due to centrifugal force (the outward force caused by spinning).

Astronomers say the asteroid's rapid spin suggests it is made of solid rock rather than loose rubble, because a pile of debris would fly apart at such speeds. The finding was possible thanks to the observatory's powerful camera, which can detect tiny changes in brightness. Future studies will help scientists learn more about how asteroids form and evolve.
