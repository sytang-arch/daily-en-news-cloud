1. Serbian Students Surge to Power After Election Defeat of Strongman Vucic

The result came less than a year after a similar uprising in Hungary removed Prime Minister Viktor Orban from power. The student groups, which began as small campus protests against corruption, grew into a nationwide movement that promised cleaner government and more jobs for young people.

Vucic, who had controlled most media and state institutions, conceded defeat late on Sunday and said he would not challenge the result. European Union officials welcomed the outcome and said they looked forward to working with the new government on reforms needed for Serbia's membership talks. Analysts said the vote showed that young voters, many of whom had never participated in elections before, can change the direction of a country when they organize peacefully and stay focused on clear demands.

Brazilian financial markets fell sharply on Monday after the surprise re-election of leftist President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva. The currency, the real, lost more than 10% of its value against the dollar, and the main stock index dropped by a similar amount, with some individual stocks sinking as much as 50%. Investors had bet that a more business-friendly candidate would win and had bought Brazilian assets in the weeks before the vote.

The sell-off (a wave of selling that pushes prices down) spread to other emerging markets as traders worried about higher government spending and larger budget deficits under Lula. The central bank said it was ready to raise interest rates to protect the currency, while the finance ministry promised to keep public finances under control. Many foreign investors are now waiting to see whether the new government will follow through on its campaign promises of more social spending.

5. Rubin Telescope Expected to Find More of Earth's Mysterious 'Mini-Moons'

Earth sometimes captures small asteroids (space rocks) that temporarily orbit our planet before flying away, and astronomers call these objects 'mini-moons.' A new study says the Rubin Observatory, a large telescope in Chile that will begin a ten-year survey of the sky next year, should find many more of them. Some mini-moons could come close enough for spacecraft to visit and collect samples, giving scientists a cheap way to study material left over from the formation of the solar system.

Mini-moons are hard to spot because they are tiny, dark and move quickly across the sky. The Rubin telescope's huge camera and wide field of view will let it scan the whole sky every few nights, making it much easier to catch these short-lived visitors. Researchers estimate that the survey could discover dozens of mini-moons, and a few may stay in orbit long enough for a small, low-cost mission to reach them and bring back pieces for study in laboratories on Earth.

6. Chinese Scientists Discover 'D-rex,' a Giant Fungus with Roots 65 Million Years Old

Scientists in China have discovered a giant new species of fungus that grows up to 20 inches (about 50 centimeters) tall and weighs nearly five pounds (over two kilograms), making it the largest known fungus of its kind. The team named it 'D-rex,' a playful reference to Tyrannosaurus rex, because of its size and its ancient origins. The fungus was found in a forest in southwestern China, where researchers collected several samples for genetic testing.

DNA analysis showed that the evolutionary lineage (family line) of D-rex appeared shortly after the dinosaurs died out about 66 million years ago, when a huge asteroid hit Earth and changed the climate. The discovery suggests that some fungi grew much larger after that event, possibly because they had more dead plants to feed on. The team says the finding helps explain how life on land recovered and changed after one of the biggest extinctions in Earth's history.
