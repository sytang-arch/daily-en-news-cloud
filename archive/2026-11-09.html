5. The Wasps That Horrified Darwin Reveal Evolution's One-Way Streets

Darwin wasps are parasitic insects that lay their eggs on or inside living hosts, such as caterpillars, beetles and spiders. Their larvae then feed on the host, slowly killing it. Charles Darwin found this lifestyle so disturbing that he questioned whether a kind creator could have designed such cruelty, and the example helped shape his thinking about natural selection.

A new study suggests that once a species evolves this parasitic lifestyle, it rarely goes back to simple hunting. The researchers compared the family trees of thousands of wasp species and found that the parasitic strategy is an 'evolutionary dead end' (a path that is almost never reversed). This supports the idea that some evolutionary changes are one-way streets, because the genes and behaviours needed for a free-living life are lost over time.

6. Air Holes Turn Silicon Wafers Into Flat Ultraviolet Focusing Mirrors for Compact Optics

Traditional lenses and mirrors focus light using curved surfaces, but flat optical devices called metasurfaces can do the same job with a thin layer of tiny structures. A research team has now created a flat mirror that works in the ultraviolet (UV) range of light, which has shorter wavelengths than visible light. The mirror is made by drilling a pattern of air holes into a silicon wafer.

The holes are arranged so that they control the phase (the timing of light waves) across the surface, allowing the mirror to focus UV light without any curve. This could lead to smaller and simpler instruments for medical testing, environmental sensing and space research. The team says the design is compatible with standard chip-making methods, which may make it easier to produce at a larger scale.
